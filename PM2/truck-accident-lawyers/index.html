When an 80,000-Pound Truck Hits You, the Stakes Are Enormous

Fully loaded commercial tractor-trailers can weigh up to 80,000 pounds — more than 20 times the weight of an average passenger car. When these massive vehicles collide with smaller vehicles, the results are almost always catastrophic: traumatic brain injuries, spinal cord damage, multiple fractures, internal bleeding, and fatalities. Federal Trucking Regulations

Commercial trucking is one of the most heavily regulated industries in the United States. The Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration (FMCSA) imposes strict rules designed to protect the public. When carriers violate these rules, they are liable for the harm that results.

Hours of Service Rules

FMCSA limits how many consecutive hours a commercial driver can operate a truck. Violations of these limits frequently cause fatigue-related crashes, and electronic logging devices are now required to track compliance.

Weight & Load Limits

Federal law caps truck weight at 80,000 pounds on interstate highways. Overloaded trucks have longer stopping distances, are more prone to rollovers, and can cause greater damage on impact.

Driver Qualification Standards

Carriers must ensure drivers hold valid commercial driver’s licenses (CDLs), meet medical certification requirements, and pass drug and alcohol testing. Negligent hiring is a serious liability issue.

Vehicle Inspection & Maintenance

Trucking companies must conduct pre-trip inspections and maintain detailed maintenance logs. Brake failures, tire blowouts, and lighting defects due to deferred maintenance are actionable safety violations.

Who Can Be Held Liable?

One of the defining complexities of truck accident cases is the number of potentially responsible parties. Unlike a simple car accident, a truck crash may involve a web of corporations, contractors, and third-party vendors each with their own insurance policies.

The Truck Driver

Drivers who violate traffic laws, exceed hours-of-service limits, drive impaired, or operate negligently bear personal liability for the accidents they cause.

The Trucking Company

Carriers are liable for negligent hiring, inadequate training, failure to supervise, and violations of federal safety regulations. They may also be vicariously liable for their drivers’ actions.

Cargo Loaders & Shippers

Improperly loaded or secured cargo can cause trailers to roll over or cargo to spill onto roadways. The parties responsible for loading may share liability.

Vehicle & Parts Manufacturers

Defective brakes, tires, steering components, or coupling systems may make a manufacturer liable under product liability theories regardless of driver conduct.

Maintenance Contractors

Third-party mechanics who perform negligent repairs or fail to identify safety-critical defects during inspections may bear liability for resulting accidents.

Leasing Companies

Under FMCSA regulations, companies that lease equipment to carriers may share liability when the leased vehicle’s condition contributes to an accident.

Common Causes of Truck Accidents

Our investigations consistently reveal a core set of preventable causes behind commercial truck crashes in the Houston area:

Driver Fatigue

Despite federal hours-of-service rules, delivery pressure and falsified logbooks lead to dangerously fatigued drivers operating 40-ton vehicles at highway speeds.

Distracted Driving

Smartphones, in-cab GPS units, dispatch communications, and other distractions are particularly deadly at the speeds and weights commercial trucks travel.

Improper Loading

Shifted cargo changes a truck’s center of gravity and can cause sudden, uncontrollable swerving or rollover, especially during lane changes and curves.

Brake Failure

Commercial truck braking systems are complex and subject to significant wear. Deferred maintenance on brake pads, air lines, and anti-lock systems is a leading cause of rear-end catastrophes.
